The ₹12 Lakh Cliff

How three AIs, one outdated rule book, and a four day deadline taught me what it means to use AI for a decision that actually mattered.

By Prudhvi Krovvidi · Published May 2026 · 12 min read
A note before we begin

The "I" in this story is a composite protagonist, not a self portrait. The salary (₹13.5 lakh), the specific rupee figures, the four named personas later in the piece, and the closing submission are all synthetic, chosen to illustrate the cliff dynamic cleanly. What is real: the AI workflow, the three model iteration, the rule updates that landed on 1 April 2026 under Income Tax Rules 2026, the prompts, and the lessons. The numbers exist to carry the story. The method is the point.

It is a Tuesday in May, and I have a deadline.

In four days, my company's tax declaration window closes. The system is called Darwinbox. The decision is called FBP, the Flexible Benefits PlanA corporate construct in India that lets salaried employees route part of their pay into tax-exempt buckets like meal cards, gift vouchers, NPS contributions, and fuel reimbursements. Done well, it can wipe out an entire year's tax bill., which lets salaried Indians reroute part of their pay into tax exempt buckets. Done well, it can eliminate an entire year's tax bill. Done poorly, it locks money into restricted use cards for no benefit at all.

I have done it badly before. This time I plan to ask an AI.

Part One. The Cliff.

For those of us under India's New Tax Regime, the tax system behaves like a cliff. A taxable income of ₹11,99,999 carries a tax bill of zero. A taxable income of ₹12,00,001 does not. The 87A rebate, the provision that wipes tax to nil below the threshold, vanishes the moment you cross it.

The drop is not gradual. Between ₹12 lakh and roughly ₹12.71 lakh, a half measure called marginal relief partially softens the fall. Past that point, the full slab tax applies and keeps climbing. At ₹13.5 lakh of gross salary, with the standard deduction of ₹75,000 applied, the bill lands close to ₹74,000 a year.

The cliff is real. It is also tunable. Every rupee you route through FBP is a rupee that does not count toward taxable income. The math is unforgiving but the levers are within reach.

Part Two. The Confident Wrong Answer.

Claude answers fast. The maximum allowed on the meal card is ₹26,400 per year, ₹2,200 per month, derived from a rule that permits ₹50 per meal across two meals a day for twenty two working days in a month. The gift voucher cap is ₹5,000 per year, set by Rule 3(7)(iv)A provision in the Income Tax Rules, 1962, governing the perquisite valuation of gifts, vouchers, and tokens given by employers to employees. Limit: ₹5,000 aggregate per financial year. of the Income Tax Rules of 1962. Together they bring the annual tax down from ₹74,000 to roughly ₹45,000.

A win. I almost stop there.

Then I ask Claude a second question. Not "is this right" but "verify every claim with web search."

The internet has news.

On the first of April, six weeks before my Tuesday, the Income Tax Department issued new rules. The meal voucher limit quadrupled. ₹50 per meal became ₹200. The annual cap rose from ₹26,400 to ₹1,05,600. The restriction that confined the benefit to the old tax regime, the one with HRA and home loan deductions, was quietly deleted. The exemption now applied to the new regime too. The one I was on.

The first answer had been mathematically correct against the wrong rule book. Claude knew the framework. It did not know the framework had been replaced.

The first AI was not wrong because it was bad. It was wrong because it was trained on the world before April.

I rewrote my plan. I felt good about the new plan. Then I pasted it into ChatGPT, just to see if it agreed.

Part Three. The Risk That Wasn't Math.

ChatGPT agreed with the math. But it did not agree with the plan.

The risk it flagged was not in any formula. It was an implementation gap. Even if the law allowed meal cards to be tax exempt under the new regime, my company's payroll system had to be configured to treat them that way. If the system still classified the card as taxable, the entire optimization would collapse. Money would be locked into a restricted use card and I would pay the same tax anyway.

A different category of mistake. Not in the math, but in the world. Get HR to confirm before submitting, it said. Send a specific email. Wait for a specific answer.

I sent the email. I kept iterating. Then I pasted the plan into Gemini.

Part Four. The Rule Rename.

Gemini found something different. The gift voucher limit, the one Claude had pegged at ₹5,000 from Rule 3(7)(iv) of 1962, had also changed on the first of April. The new rules renumbered the provision to Rule 15(5)(a) and tripled the cap from ₹5,000 to ₹15,000.

Claude, with web search, had caught the meal card update. It had missed this one. ChatGPT had not noticed either. Gemini did, with the rule number attached and three publications cited.

Three AIs. Three pieces of the answer. None of them complete on its own.

Part Five. What This Says About AI.

Here is the thing about training data. It freezes. Claude's most recent knowledge stops months before the rules I needed. Web search closes some of the gap but not all of it, because the internet does not have a single canonical version of any update, and Claude's web index has its own coverage holes. Gemini's training freezes at a different point and surfaces a different subset of the public record. ChatGPT is different again.

The mental model many people carry, that AI is a smart friend who knows the latest thing, is wrong. AI is many smart friends with overlapping but non identical knowledge of the past, none of them with perfect coverage of the present.

For decisions involving rules that change, asking one AI is like asking one friend. Probably useful. Probably incomplete. Occasionally, dangerously confident in the wrong answer.

Multi model verification is not paranoia. It is the cost of taking AI seriously.

Part Six. Four People, One Tuesday.

This is not just my story. The optimization depends on what bracket you sit in. Four synthetic people, none of them real, all of them facing the same question, all of them arriving at different correct answers.

Part Seven. The Number That Lands.

The number that should land hardest: ₹74,000 saved in four days of iteration, using free tools, with the first AI giving the wrong answer.

That is not a rounding error. That is a signal about how to use these tools for anything that matters.

The first AI was confident. The second was confident. The third was confident. The truth was a composite no single one of them held.
Part Eight. What Changes.

For any decision that involves rules that change, this is the new minimum. Ask the AI. Then ask it to verify against the web. Then paste the answer into a second AI for review. Then look for a real world artifact that confirms or refutes the claim.

My company's FBP form already had the new caps baked in. The form was the answer. I just took the long route to find it.

If a single AI is enough for your decision, the decision probably did not need an AI.

What I would want to confirm.

The plan works only if my company's payroll actually processes the meal card as tax exempt under the new regime. The law says it should. The form suggests it will. The HR email implies it does. The proof arrives at the end of June, when the TDS line on my payslip should drop to zero. If it does not, I will know something in the implementation chain broke, and I will have a different story to tell.

This is what intellectual honesty looks like under deadlines. State the confidence level. Name the test. Move forward.

It is a Tuesday in May. I have one day left on my deadline. I have submitted enough to the meal card and the gift voucher to bring my taxable income just under the cliff. My tax is zero.

The work was iteration. The tools were free. The lesson was older than the tools.

Trust, but cross verify.
