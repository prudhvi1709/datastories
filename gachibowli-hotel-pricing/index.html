The hotel that doubled its price, and nobody blinked

What seven months of room rates reveal about who holds power in a market, who quietly surrenders it, and why the discount tag is the most honest number on the page.

A data story · Seven snapshots, June 2026 to January 2027

On a weekday in September 2026, a single hotel on the edge of Hyderabad's tech corridor asked Rupees 38,260 for one night's sleep. Three months earlier, the same hotel, the same rooms, the same lobby, the same staff, had asked Rupees 17,000. Nothing about the building had changed. Its guest rating sat unmoved at 4.1. Its review count hadn't ticked up in any meaningful way. And yet the price had more than doubled.

Now imagine you are the traveller staring at that screen. A room two streets away, clean and well-reviewed, is going for a tenth of the price. Most of us would scroll on. But enough people don't, and that single fact, that some guests will pay almost anything while others vanish at the first rupee of markup, is the engine beneath the entire hotel market. Economists have a cold, clinical name for it. We will get there. First, the mystery.

If the rooms didn't change, and the reviews didn't change, what exactly were guests paying double for?

Beat One · The world as we assume it

Everyone expects prices to move together

Here is the comfortable assumption. Gachibowli is a business district. Business districts fill up on weekdays and empty on holidays. So you would expect the hotels here to rise and fall as one tide, lifting together for the festive rush, easing together when the city goes quiet. A rising sea, the saying goes, lifts all boats.

We tracked the asking price of the same set of hotels across seven moments in time, from the summer of 2026 to the depths of the following winter. If the comfortable assumption holds, the lines on the next chart should rise and fall in loose formation, like a flock of birds banking together.

They do not.

Beat Two · The crack in the wisdom

The same season, wildly different answers

Look at what the festive stretch does to each hotel and the comfortable assumption falls apart. From June to its September peak, the five-star Hyatt swings upward by 125 percent. Down the road, the four-star Ellaa nudges its rate by all of 11 percent across the same window. One hotel treats the calendar as a license to print money. The other barely lifts a finger.

And then there is the third kind of hotel, the one that does the opposite of raising prices. The Treebo properties carry a sticker price near Rupees 18,000 and sell, night after night, for around Rupees 7,000. They are not pricing for the festive surge. They are bracing against it.

Three hotels, three philosophies, one street. The festive season is not a tide that lifts them together. It is a test, and each hotel answers it differently. The question is why, and the answer is the most useful idea in all of pricing.

Beat Three · The idea underneath

It comes down to one question: who can walk away?

Economists call it price elasticity, but you can forget the term and keep the picture. Every hotel faces a simple, brutal question. If I raise my price, how many guests walk away?

For the Hyatt, the answer is: almost none. Its guests are consultants on expense accounts, executives flying in for a board meeting, families who decided months ago that this is the place. They are not comparison-shopping at midnight. So the Hyatt can double its rate and watch its rating hold steady at 4.1, because the people who would have complained never left. That is an inelastic hotel, and inelastic hotels charge what they like.

That's not a rounding error. That's a signal. The discount tag is a hotel confessing how scared it is of losing you.

For Treebo, the answer is the reverse. Raise the price by a few hundred rupees and the booking evaporates to the cleaner, cheaper room next door. So Treebo never raises it. It slashes the sticker by nearly two-thirds and competes on the only axis it trusts: cheapness. That is an elastic hotel, and elastic hotels chase volume because yield was never theirs to chase.

Here is the quiet revelation. You do not need booking volumes or occupancy reports to see which hotel is which. The discount depth already tells you. A hotel holding firm at zero percent off is a hotel that knows you'll pay. A hotel screaming 64 percent off is a hotel terrified you won't. The price tag is a mood ring.

Drag the elastic hotel's price upward and the revenue curve crests, then collapses, the guests fleeing faster than the price can climb. Drag the inelastic hotel and the curve just keeps rising, which is exactly why the Hyatt keeps reaching for higher numbers. Think of it like two shopkeepers: one sells diamonds, one sells umbrellas. The diamond seller can name a price; the umbrella seller prays for rain. Though unlike umbrellas, a hotel's elasticity shifts with the season, which is the next twist.

The mechanism · Where the overflow goes

When the giants spike, the spillover has to land somewhere

There is a second force at work, and it connects the hotels that look unconnected. When the Hyatt and the Sheraton vault past Rupees 30,000 in the festive crush, they don't just earn more. They push a wave of priced-out travellers downhill, toward the budget rooms. Watch the demand flow.

Each dot is a traveller who wanted the Hyatt and booked the Treebo instead. The budget hotels don't need to advertise during the festive rush. The giants advertise for them.

This is why a budget hotel can hold its rate flat all year and still fill in October. Its demand isn't really about its own price. It's about the giant up the road deciding to get greedy. The hotels that look like rivals are, in this moment, business partners, one harvesting the wealthy, the other catching everyone it drops.

Beat Four · So what changes

The money is hiding in the gaps between the lines

If you run one of these hotels, the story stops being interesting and starts being actionable. Three moves fall out of the data.

For the inelastic hotels, look down, not up. The Hyatt has mastered the climb. What it may be missing is the floor. In the August lull it still asked Rupees 26,000, a number set by habit rather than demand. The opportunity isn't a higher peak. It's a smarter trough, shaving the off-season rate to capture the budget-conscious traveller who would happily trade up to a five-star at the right price, then snapping back to rack rate the moment the season turns.

For the elastic hotels, stop apologizing. A hotel that lives at 60 percent off has trained its guests to wait for the cut. The October festive window, when the giants spike and the overflow comes looking, is the one moment its demand turns inelastic for it. That is the week to quietly trim the discount, not deepen it. The travellers arriving then have nowhere cheaper to go.

For everyone, watch the supply gauge. The number of rooms on offer fell from 348 in June to 252 in January, a quarter of the market simply gone. Fewer rooms is the cleanest early-warning signal that pricing power is shifting toward whoever stays bookable. When supply tightens, hesitation is expensive.

The next move isn't a bigger spreadsheet. It's a daily question: who can walk away today, and who can't?

Behind the curtain · how we got the numbers

No survey, no API. A browser agent that booked like a human

A story is only as trustworthy as its evidence, so here is exactly how the evidence was gathered. There was no special data feed and no insider access. The numbers came the same way a guest's would: by opening a booking site and looking.

1

A browser agent searched like a real traveller

An automated browser agent opened the hotel booking site and ran the exact search a guest would: area set to Gachibowli, one room, two adults, one night, sorted by price low to high, filtered to rooms above Rupees 3,000. No scraping of hidden endpoints. Just the page everyone sees.

2

It captured seven snapshots across the seasons

The same search was repeated for seven check-in dates spanning summer, monsoon, the festive surge, Christmas, and the post-holiday recovery, holding the search constant so price was the only thing free to move.

3

For each hotel it recorded the honest fields

Name, star rating, property type, the price shown, the struck-through MRP, the discount percentage, the guest rating, and the review count, plus its rank on the page, which doubles as a rough demand signal.

4

Then it watched the same hotels over time

By matching hotels across snapshots, the price journeys emerged: who climbed, who held, who discounted. Those journeys are every line and bar in this story. The discount field, captured for free, turned out to be the richest signal of all.

What we'd want to confirm

One honest limit, stated plainly. We are reading asking prices, not bookings. A high rate proves a hotel's confidence, not that the rooms sold. To turn this from a strong signal into a settled fact, the next step is occupancy data: did the Hyatt's Rupees 38,260 rooms actually fill? Everything here is robust as a read on pricing behaviour. Pair it with even one month of occupancy and it becomes a read on demand itself. That is the experiment worth running next.

The hotel that doubled its price wasn't being reckless. It was answering the only question that matters, and answering it correctly: this week, its guests cannot walk away. Down the street, the hotel at 64 percent off was answering the same question with the opposite, equally correct, reply. Once you see the question, you cannot unsee it on any price tag again.
